Self-Improving Agents

Self-improving agents: a map of the field

Dan Li · November 2026 · 8 min read

The literature on recursive self-improvement roughly doubled this fall. Of the 173 arXiv records that mentioned it through mid-October, 106 were posted after September 1. There are now at least six surveys, over 40 benchmarks, and more than 70 ICLR 2027 submissions on evolving agent harnesses alone.

I've spent the last few weeks reading through it for a project with Xunjian Yin. This post is my map of it. It covers one idea that makes the field easier to read, how the field got here, what each part of it is doing, what the audits found, and what I think is true.

  • Almost everything called "self-improving" runs on frozen weights and edits the harness: prompts, memory, skills, tools, control code.
  • The gains are real but inflated. When you match compute, use held-out tasks and repeat runs, a large share of them disappears.
  • Nobody has yet shown a system that gets better at improving itself.

One ladder to read everything

The surveys use different names but agree on the structure. Self-improvement means the system makes a persistent, inherited change to itself. Improving the current answer, as Self-Refine or Reflexion do, doesn't count. Above that, the claims form a ladder:

Order 0The product is better. The final agent scores higher.
Order 1The improver is better. From the same start, on the same budget, a later improver produces better successors than an earlier one.
Order 2The improver of the improver is better.

"Recursive" means order 1 or higher. Duan et al. split it into structural recursion, where a revised mechanism governs later rounds, and effective recursion, where that mechanism actually produces better successors in a fair comparison. Nearly every paper below makes an order-0 claim, and order 2 has never been measured. Most of the confusion in this field comes from describing an order-0 result in order-1 language.

How we got here

Two seeds2023

One seed was in-task refinement (Self-Refine, Reflexion). The other was search over prompts and programs (OPRO, DSPy). Promptbreeder evolved its own mutation prompts, and STOP had an improver rewrite itself, scored by what it later produced. That was the first order-1 objective. STOP's gains appeared with GPT-4 and reversed with weaker models.

Meta-agents2024

ADAS named the problem of automated agent design: a frozen meta-agent writes agent code, conditioned on an archive. AFlow and AgentSquare followed. Gödel Agent made the designer itself self-modifying.

The archive era2025

The Darwin Gödel Machine replaced the Gödel Machine's proof with an empirical archive and took SWE-bench from 20% to 50%. GEPA, ACE and ReasoningBank made prompts and memory into learnable objects. Absolute Zero and SEAL pushed the loop into the weights.

The harness becomes the unitearly 2026

Meta-Harness gave a coding agent filesystem access to every prior harness, score and trace. It found raw traces beat scores alone, 56.7 vs 41.3. Hyperagents put the task agent and the meta-agent in one editable program.

Critiquemid 2026

Random sampling matched automated agent search. CoT with self-consistency beat automated multi-agent systems at under a tenth of the cost. The first matched-budget audit found harness evolution losing to plain parallel sampling.

The boomSep–Oct 2026

New work appeared in several directions at once: improver-level systems, harness–weight co-evolution, research agents that improve research agents, and dozens of benchmarks.

The measurement turnOct–Nov 2026

The strongest recent papers are audits, swap tests and nested replays. They check whether the loops work instead of building new ones.

The map

Here are seven areas of the field. For each one: what it is, where it stands, and what's still open.

Harness evolutioncrowded

The weights stay frozen. A proposer, usually a coding agent, edits the scaffold using traces and scores. Same-model gains on Terminal-Bench are large: AHE goes from 69.7 to 77.0 and DarwinX from 75.5 to 83.2. The gains are biggest on weak models, and they come from structural edits (tools, middleware) more than from prose. The field still has no shared evaluation contract: no matched test-time-compute baseline, no sealed held-out set, and no requirement for several seeds.

Meta-agents & workflow designcrowded

A meta-agent designs other agents. Reported averages on the AFlow suite climbed from 80 to 89 in a year, but the numbers aren't comparable across papers, and reproductions put ADAS at or below plain CoT. In AgentSquare's own table, a random combination of modules beats every hand-designed agent on 4 of 6 tasks. How you represent the design space matters as much as how you search it.

Prompts, memory & skillscrowded

Here the agent edits the text it reads at run time (GEPA, ACE, ReasoningBank, skill libraries). The in-domain gains are real. But a single 2,048-token note matches the elaborate loops with about a third of the tokens. After a silent tool change, stale experience drops success from 63% to 23%. Only 5 of 21 improved skills keep their full gain on held-out tasks. The open problem is deciding what to admit, keep and retire.

Improver-level RSIhot

These systems run two loops: the inner loop improves the artifact, and the outer loop edits or trains the improver. The core question is the meta-reward: how do you credit an edit whose value only shows up in later successors? About eight forms exist (one-step utility, clade credit, matched replay, lookahead, statistical gates), and no paper compares them head to head. Left alone, improvers barely edit themselves, in 1–15% of generations, so most systems now force meta-updates on a schedule.

Harness ↔ weightssaturated

These papers co-evolve the harness and the weights, or distill harness behavior into the weights. Optimizing only one side usually leaves gains on the table, and naive alternation can collapse; one run fell from 90.5 to 31.5. There's a neat regularity here. A supportive harness helps weight learning when it only steps in after every rollout fails, and hides learning when it's always on. No one has a compute-matched multi-round breakdown, so we don't know if these loops compound.

Self-play & label-free RLcrowded

The system writes its own problems, opponents or environments (Absolute Zero, R-Zero, SPICE, SPADE). Pure closed loops plateau and then collapse within 3–10 rounds. A little outside grounding delays that: a corpus, 50 human anchors, or a code executor. Label-free RL tells the same story. Intrinsic rewards sharpen what the model already knows: in one test-time RL audit, 0.7% of problems were newly learned and 21.6% got worse.

AI research agentshot

These are agents doing ML R&D. On Singularity-Bench, Opus 5.5 with Claude Code scores 1.25 against production human baselines on narrow tasks. The gains come from engineering tricks, not insight, and 30.5% of open-ended runs reward-hack without being prompted to. AIDE²'s "ignition test" asked whether a self-improved research agent is a better improver. The result was 0.780 vs 0.782, which the authors call inconclusive.

The measurement turn

Here is what happened when people re-checked the headline claims:

ClaimOn re-check
Harness evolution beats baselinesLost to parallel sampling at matched budget, 67.4 vs 72.3, with zero held-out gain. Rethinking-Eval
Reported harness gainsOn average, 54% of the gain is overfitting to the search set and 14% is test-time scaling. HDA
Elaborate memory loopsMatched by a 2,048-token note at 65–71% fewer tokens. RSI Tax
Keep-if-better selectionPicks the better candidate 49.7% of the time. In-loop gains of 6.9–9.0 pp re-evaluate to between +0.4 and −3.2. Resolution Hypothesis
Lineages compoundGains mostly come from inheriting one early edit. Successor gain is δ₁ = +0.003. Improvement by Inheritance
Improvers get betterOnly 4 of 26 such claims compare like with like. Auditing the Product

The shared diagnosis is that generation is cheap and selection is the bottleneck. Per-edit effects are a few points, while evaluation sets have 16–50 items and one rollout each. You can't select between candidates you can't tell apart.

The key method is the swap test, or nested replay. Watching one lineage can't tell you whether the improver got better, because later improvers also inherit better artifacts. You have to start an early improver and a late improver from the same artifact, give them the same budget, and compare what they produce. Auditing the Product proves that trajectory data alone can't identify improver gain.

The evaluator is the soft spot

A loop optimizes whatever signal it can read. Some examples:

  • An always-pass grader trains a skill loop almost as well as a valid one, within 0.038.
  • Agents succeed 97% of the time in a synthesized simulator, but only 3% of those action sequences work when replayed in the real environment.
  • Evolved agents fail 43.9% of SEABench safety tests, and their non-evolving twins fail none.
  • A poisoned benchmark survives clean re-evolution. Frontier agents can plant covert properties in their successors.
  • DGM "objective-hacked" its own hallucination detector.

The field agrees that safety needs something outside the loop's read/write set: a sealed test set, a frozen core, a rollback copy, or a human anchor. Nobody knows how to certify a self-evolved evaluator without one.

Where it's heading

  • Order-1 evidence becomes the bar. I'd expect NeurIPS 2027 reviewers to ask for a swap test on any "better improver" claim.
  • A standard evaluation contract with a sealed held-out set, a matched test-time-scaling baseline, a bounded-note baseline, at least 3 seeds, and an independent grader.
  • Noise-aware selection (statistical gates, winner's-curse corrections) becomes its own method lane.
  • Drifting, continual settings replace static benchmarks. We'll get the first tests of whether an agent that has self-improved for k rounds learns new tasks worse than a fresh one.
  • Labs report research agents improving research agents. The first claimed order-1 positive result will be contested.

My takes

  1. Stop building loops; build controls. A new loop without a matched-compute baseline is mostly measuring test-time scaling. The most useful paper right now is a cheap design that shows where self-improvement breaks.
  2. Flip the compute budget. Loops spend almost everything on generating candidates and almost nothing on deciding between them. A few candidates evaluated well should beat a big archive scored on 30 questions.
  3. Call order 0 what it is. Harness optimization works, and it's useful engineering. But it isn't recursive self-improvement unless the improver changes and that change pays off.
  4. The low self-edit rate is a finding. Improvers that are allowed to edit themselves rarely do, and when they do, the edits don't help. That tells us something about current models. Forcing edits on a schedule is the right way to study it, but it doesn't make the finding go away.
  5. The base model is the biggest self-improvement. Swapping the optimizer model moves the gain about 1.8× more than swapping the harness. A harness gain that disappears when the model changes is a patch, not learning. Harness methods should be judged on whether they transfer across models.
  6. I ran into this myself. In a small pilot, Coaching the Coach, I evolved a HyperAgents-style coach for ARC-AGI-2 solvers and measured it across generations. It didn't produce better solvers, and letting the solver think longer beat it. It's one small run, but it matches what the audits found.
  7. Hide something from the loop. The ground truth has to sit where the loop can't read or write it. That's both the safety argument and the science argument: if the evaluator is inside the loop, you can't tell improvement from gaming.
